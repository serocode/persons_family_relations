Part One — Preliminary Title, Chapter 1: Effect & Application of Laws

When Laws Take Effect (CC 2)

Tañada v. Tuvera
GR 63915 (29 Dec 1986)

Publication of laws in the Official Gazette is indispensable to due process. A clause stating a law is effective "immediately" only fixes the reference point for counting from publication — it does not waive the publication requirement itself.

Villafuerte v. Cordial, Jr.
GR 222450 (7 July 2020)

Not every issuance needs publication. Interpretative regulations and purely internal rules — regulating only agency personnel, not the public, such as letters of instruction to subordinates on how to perform their duties — need not be published to take effect or to be valid.

DENREU v. Abad
GR 204152 (19 Jan 2021)

Administrative issuances, such as DBM circulars enforcing salary or allowance adjustments that affect the general public or rights of government employees, must be published in the Official Gazette or a newspaper of general circulation to be effective and enforceable.

Non-Retroactivity of Laws (CC 4)

Liam Law v. Olympic Sawmill Co.
GR L-30771 (1984)

A curative statute validating a previously usurious/defective loan stipulation may be applied retroactively to a pending case, since remedial statutes validating rather than invalidating a transaction do not impair a vested right.

SMC v. CIR
GR 257697 (12 April 2023)

Pursuant to Section 246 of the NIRC and Article 4 of the Civil Code, BIR rulings and administrative issuances cannot be applied retroactively if doing so would prejudice taxpayers who relied on previous rulings in good faith.

Mandatory v. Prohibitory Laws (CC 5)

UOBP v. Board of Commissioners
GR 182133 (23 June 2015)

Under Article 5 of the Civil Code, acts executed against mandatory or prohibitory laws are void. A mortgage executed over a subdivision or condominium unit without prior written approval from HLURB as required by Section 18 of P.D. 957 is null and void.

Waiver of Rights (CC 6)

Doña Adela Export International v. TIDCORP
GR 201931 (11 Feb 2015)

A waiver of a right (here, bank-deposit confidentiality) contained in a compromise agreement is not valid against a party who neither consented to it nor was a signatory — a waiver only binds those who actually made it.

Spouses Garcia v. Spouses Soriano
GR 219431 (24 Aug 2020)

Under Article 6 of the Civil Code, a waiver of a right must be clear, positive, and unequivocal, executed with full knowledge of the existence of the right being renounced; an intention to waive ownership or legal remedies cannot be lightly presumed.

Termination of Effectivity of Laws (CC 7)

DPWH v. PICE
GR 200015 (15 March 2023)

Implied repeals are strongly disfavored under Article 7 of the Civil Code. A general law (such as the National Building Code) does not repeal or modify a prior special law (such as the Architecture Act or Civil Engineering Law) unless there is an irreconcilable inconsistency or explicit statutory intent.

Bitanagan Farmers v. Hacienda Bitanagan
GR 243310 (15 Aug 2022)

When a statute or legal program expires or is superseded, rights already vested while the law was in effect remain preserved, but new claims cannot be asserted under a non-existent or expired statutory mechanism.

Judicial Decisions: Stare Decisis (CC 8)

De Roy v. Court of Appeals
GR 80718 (1988)

A Supreme Court decision forms part of the legal system from the date of its promulgation; a party's or counsel's unawareness of a doctrine because it had not yet appeared in the official reports (SCRA) is not an excuse.

Pesca v. Pesca
GR 136921 (17 April 2001)

The guidelines in Republic v. Molina for proving Article 36 psychological incapacity may be applied even to a petition filed before Molina was decided, since judicial interpretation of a law forms part of that law as of the date of its enactment, not the date of the interpreting decision.

Duty to Render Judgment (CC 9, 10)

Alonzo v. Intermediate Appellate Court
GR L-72873 (28 May 1987)

Where a co-owner had actual, undisputed knowledge of a sale, this may be treated as equivalent to the written notice the law requires to start the period for legal redemption — the Court applied the law's underlying purpose over its strict letter.

People v. Tulagan
GR 227363 (12 March 2019)

Harmonized the overlapping and previously inconsistent penalty schemes of the Revised Penal Code and R.A. 7610 for sexual abuse/lascivious conduct against minors of different age brackets, laying down a definitive penalty matrix where the statutes' interplay had been unclear.

Computation of Time / Legal Periods (CC 13)

Abello v. Banco Filipino
GR 232706, Resolution (18 July 2022)

In computing legal periods under Article 13 of the Civil Code and the Rules of Court, if the last day of a period falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or legal holiday, the time shall not run until the next working day.

Quiqui v. Boncaros
GR L-51841 (1987)

Under Article 13 of the Civil Code, in counting a legal period (such as the 30-day redemption period under Art. 1623), the first day is excluded and the last day is included; if notice is received on a given date, counting begins on the day immediately following.

Conflicts Rules (CC 14–18) — Territoriality, Nationality, Lex Situs, Renvoi

Del Socorro v. Van Wilsem
GR 193707 (10 Dec 2014)

R.A. 9262 (VAWC) is a territorial penal statute enforceable against a foreign national father for denying support to his child in the Philippines; absent proof of a contrary Dutch law, the processual presumption (foreign law presumed same as Philippine law) applies.

Suzuki v. OSG
GR 212302 (2 Sept 2020)

Philippine courts do not take judicial notice of foreign laws or foreign judgments affecting status; under nationality principle (Art. 15) and Rule 108, a foreign divorce or change in status must be pleaded and proven as a fact before the civil registry can be updated.

IPAMS v. De Vera
GR 205703 (7 March 2016)

Party autonomy to stipulate a foreign law governing an overseas Filipino worker's employment contract is not absolute; it must be expressly and validly agreed upon and cannot be used to render Philippine labor-protection laws illusory (citing Pakistan International Airlines and Sameer Overseas).

Aznar v. Garcia (the "Christensen" case)
7 SCRA 95 (1963)

The classic renvoi case: California's own conflicts rule pointed the question of succession back to the law of the decedent's domicile (the Philippines), and the Philippine court accepted that referral back, applying Philippine internal law on legitimes.

Raytheon International v. Rouzie
GR 162894 (26 Feb 2008)

The mere presence of foreign elements in a case does not automatically warrant dismissal on the ground of forum non conveniens; it is a matter of sound judicial discretion, not an automatic bar, where the Philippine court has jurisdiction and trial here is not seriously inconvenient.

Roehr v. Rodriguez
GR 142820 (2003)

A divorce validly obtained abroad by a foreign spouse against his Filipino wife capacitates him to remarry, but Philippine courts retain jurisdiction over incidental matters like custody and property that the foreign decree did not conclusively settle.

Ambrose v. Suque Ambrose
GR 206761 (23 June 2021)

Under Article 26(2) of the Family Code, a Filipino spouse seeking judicial recognition of a foreign divorce must prove not only the foreign divorce decree but also the national law of the alien spouse capacitating them to remarry.

Part One — Preliminary Title, Chapter 2: Human Relations (CC 19–36)

Application of Arts. 19–21 in Contractual/Labor Settings

Tocoms Phils. v. Philips Electronics
GR 214046 (5 Feb 2020)

Exercising a contractual right to terminate a distributorship agreement, when done in accordance with agreed terms and without bad faith or intent to injure, does not constitute an abuse of rights under Article 19 of the Civil Code.

St. Martin v. LWV Construction
GR 217426 (4 Dec 2017)

A medical clinic that negligently issues erroneous medical exam results, causing an overseas worker's deployment to be canceled, is civilly liable for damages under Article 19 in relation to Article 2176 (quasi-delict) for breach of its duty of care.

Arco Pulp v. Lim
GR 206806 (25 June 2014)

Good faith and fairness require compliance with obligations under Article 19; where an alternative obligation is agreed upon, the choice belongs to the debtor, but once communicated or converted, the debtor cannot unilaterally evade liability to the prejudice of the creditor.

Custodio v. Court of Appeals
GR 116100 (9 Feb 1996)

Constructing a wall that legally blocks a neighbor's former passage, without malice or bad faith, is damnum absque injuria (loss without legal injury) — the exercise of one's own property rights is not actionable under Arts. 19–21 absent bad faith or intent to harm.

Duty to Act with Justice, Honesty & Good Faith

Cebu Country Club v. Elizagaque
GR 160273 (18 Jan 2008)

A private club's board may have absolute discretion to blackball a membership applicant under its by-laws, but exercising that discretion in bad faith and without giving the applicant any reason violates Art. 19's duty to act with justice and good faith, giving rise to liability.

Actions for Breach of Promise to Marry

Baksh v. Court of Appeals
GR 97336 (1993)

A mere breach of promise to marry is not actionable per se, but where the promise is attended by fraud and deceit, employed solely to obtain sexual favors, with willful intent to injure the woman's honor and reputation, damages are proper under Art. 21.

Guevara v. Banach
GR 214016, 3rd Div., Leonen, J. (24 Nov 2021)

A breach of promise to marry is not an actionable wrong per se; however, money or property given strictly in contemplation of a marriage that failed to materialize may be recovered under the principle of unjust enrichment (Art. 22), not as moral damages under Art. 21 unless accompanied by fraud or deceit.

Wassmer v. Velez
GR L-20089 (1964)

The classic case: a groom who abandoned the bride two days before an elaborately-prepared wedding, without justification, was held liable for damages under Art. 21 — not for the broken promise itself, but for the manner (bad faith, contrary to good customs) in which the wrong was done.

Unjust Enrichment

Land Bank v. Catadman
GR 200407 (2020)

To sustain a claim for unjust enrichment under Article 22, two conditions must concur: a person is enriched without a just or legal cause, and such enrichment is derived at the expense of another.

Shinryo Co. v. RRN Inc.
GR 172525 (20 Oct 2010)

The principle of unjust enrichment (Art. 22) prevents a party from retaining benefits or extra work performed by a subcontractor without paying just compensation, even in the absence of a formal written change order, based on quantum meruit.

BPI v. LCL Capital
GR 243396 (2021)

A financial institution that improperly debits or retains funds belonging to a client without legal justification is liable to return the funds under Article 22 to prevent unjust enrichment.

NPC v. Benguet Electric
GR 218378 (2021)

Unjust enrichment under Article 22 exists only when a person retains a benefit without any legal ground; it does not apply where the retention or collection of payment is grounded upon a valid statutory provision or existing contract.

Garcia v. Philippine Airlines
GR 164856 (2009, en banc)

Wages paid to illegally dismissed employees before the dismissal is finally reversed on appeal need not be refunded to the employer even if the dismissal is later upheld, since the employee rendered no unjust enrichment — he was reinstated pending appeal by operation of law and the wages corresponded to actual availability for work.

Joyfood v. Delta
GR 237366 (2020)

Unjust enrichment under Article 22 is an action of last resort (subsidiary) and cannot be invoked when the relationship, obligations, and remedies of the parties are explicitly governed by a contract or standard statutory rules.

Tortious Interference in a Contract

So Ping Bun v. Court of Appeals
GR 120554 (1999)

Interference with another's contract is not automatically tortious; a party who interferes to protect his own legitimate financial interest, without employing wrongful means, is not liable — lawful competition and self-interest can justify what would otherwise look like interference.

Court Vigilance

De Lima v. Laguna
160 SCRA 70

Under Article 24 of the Civil Code, courts are mandated to exercise vigilance for the protection of an individual who is at a disadvantage due to moral dependence, ignorance, indigence, mental weakness, or other handicap.

Human Personality (Privacy)

RCPI v. Verchez
GR 164349 (2006)

A telegraph company's negligent delay in transmitting an urgent message about a family member's worsening illness — causing the family to fail to reach her in time before her death — was actionable; the negligence, though arising from a contract of carriage of messages, supported liability for the resulting anguish under the Human Relations provisions.

Hing v. Choachuy
GR 179736 (2013)

Installing CCTV cameras aimed at a neighboring property, capturing activities within a reasonable expectation of privacy, violates the right to privacy under Art. 26 and supports an injunction, even absent an existing legal easement dispute between the parties.

Liability of Public Officers

Aberca v. Ver
GR L-69866 (1988)

Military and other public officers who violate a person's constitutional rights (illegal searches, arbitrary detention during Martial Law-era security operations) may be held civilly liable under Arts. 32 and 19–21, and cannot hide behind a superior's order or a state-of-emergency claim to escape liability.

Lim v. Ponce de Leon
L-22554 (29 Aug 1975)

Under Article 32 of the Civil Code, a public officer (such as a fiscal or police officer) who directly or indirectly violates a citizen's constitutional right against illegal search and seizure is civilly liable for damages, regardless of whether the officer acted in good faith or bad faith.

Tabuena v. Sandiganbayan
GR 103501-03 (1997)

A public officer who, in good faith, complies with a direct, facially lawful order from a superior (in this case, a presidential directive regarding a COA-audited disbursement) may be acquitted of malversation/graft where the element of intent to gain or bad faith is not independently proven.

Unfair Competition

Willaware v. Jesichris
GR 195549 (2014)

Unfair competition targets the use of unjust, oppressive, or high-handed methods that deprive others of a fair chance to compete — ordinary lawful competition, even if it takes away a rival's customers, is not by itself actionable.

Prejudicial Questions & Independent Civil Actions

Cojuangco v. Palma
Adm. Case No. 2474 (2004)

A Filipino lawyer who marries abroad without resolving a subsisting Philippine marriage commits bigamy and gross immoral conduct, violating Article 19 of the Civil Code and the lawyer's oath, warranting disbarment regardless of foreign marriage laws.

Reyes v. Pearlbank Securities
GR 171435 (2008)

Under Article 36 of the Civil Code and Rule 111, a prejudicial question requires that a previously instituted civil case involves an issue intimately tied to the criminal case, such that the civil resolution determines whether the criminal action may proceed.

Pimentel v. Pimentel
GR 172060 (2010)

A pending civil action for declaration of nullity of marriage does not constitute a prejudicial question that suspends a criminal prosecution for frustrated parricide or VAWC, as the determination of marital validity is not determinative of criminal guilt or innocence.

Pulido v. People
GR 220149, en banc, unanimous (2021)

An accused in a bigamy case may raise the defense that his first marriage was void ab initio even without a prior, separate judicial declaration of nullity — a void marriage produces no legal effect from inception, and this may be shown as a defense within the bigamy trial itself.

Book 1: Persons — Title I, Civil Personality

No cases are separately assigned in your syllabus for Juridical Capacity, Capacity to Act, Natural Persons, or Juridical Persons — this chapter is codal-provision only (Arts. 37–51) in your outline.

Family Code, Title I: Marriage — Chapter 1, Requisites

Concept

In Re: Bucana
AM 1637 (1976)

A lawyer or officer who solemnizes or participates in a marriage ceremony knowing that statutory requirements or legal capacities are violated commits administrative misconduct and gross breach of professional ethics.

Legal Capacity: Sex

Silverio v. Republic
GR 174689 (2007)

There is no Philippine law authorizing a change of the "sex" entry in the civil registry based on sex reassignment surgery; "sex" for these purposes is fixed at birth in its ordinary, biological sense, absent specific legislation.

Republic v. Cagandahan
GR 166676 (2008)

Distinguished from Silverio: where the sexual ambiguity is congenital (here, Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia) rather than surgically created, the person may, upon reaching majority, choose which sex to be legally identified with.

Legal Impediments: Prior/Subsequent Marriages

Wiegel v. Sempio-Diy
GR L-53703 (1986)

Under the old (pre-Family Code) rule, a marriage void from the beginning (e.g., due to a subsisting prior marriage) does not need a prior judicial declaration of nullity before a party can be treated as never having been validly married for certain purposes — its validity may be passed upon directly in the very proceeding where it is raised.

Ty v. Court of Appeals
GR 127406 (2000)

Similarly, a party sued for support could still have her marriage's validity (void for want of a license) passed upon by the court in that same proceeding, without needing a separate, prior action for declaration of nullity — decided under the old rule before Art. 40's stricter requirement took full effect for remarriage purposes.

Pasamonte v. Teneza
AC 11104 (2020)

A lawyer who enters into a second marriage without first obtaining a judicial declaration of nullity of a prior subsisting marriage is guilty of gross immoral conduct under the Code of Professional Responsibility, subjecting them to disbarment.

Consent

Republic v. Albios
GR 198780 (2013)

A marriage entered into so a Filipino spouse could obtain a foreign immigration benefit remains valid, since consent under Art. 2 was freely given in the proper form; a marriage is not automatically void merely because the underlying motive was to obtain an incidental benefit.

Solemnizing Officers

Beso v. Daguman
AM MTJ-99-1211 (2000)

A judge who solemnizes a marriage outside his territorial jurisdiction does not thereby void the marriage — the marriage remains valid, but the judge is administratively liable for the irregularity.

Aranes v. Occiano
AM MTJ-02-1390 (2002)

A judge who solemnizes a marriage knowing the couple has not yet obtained a marriage license — proceeding anyway based on a promise to submit it later — renders the marriage void for absence of a license at the time of the ceremony, and is himself administratively liable.

OCA v. Necessario
AM MTJ-07-1691 (2013)

The "package marriage" scandal case: judges solemnizing marriages en masse without personally verifying compliance with the essential and formal requisites (proper licenses, actual appearance, genuine Art. 34 exemption) were held administratively liable for gross neglect and gross ignorance of the law.

Marriage License

Sy v. Court of Appeals
GR 127263 (2000)

Irregularities surrounding the issuance of a marriage license (discrepancies in venue/date) do not void a marriage where a license did in fact exist and was used — courts lean toward the presumption of a valid marriage absent proof the license was altogether absent.

Alcantara v. Alcantara
GR 167746 (2007)

A discrepancy in the marriage license number appearing on the marriage contract does not void the marriage absent clear proof, from the local civil registrar, that no license was actually issued to the parties.

Abbas v. Abbas
GR 183896 (2013)

A civil registrar's certification of "no record" of a marriage license is insufficient to prove the license never existed unless the certification affirmatively shows an exhaustive search of all pertinent records was conducted.

De Castro v. De Castro
GR 160172 (2008)

Article 40's requirement of a prior judicial declaration of nullity applies specifically for purposes of remarriage; for other purposes (such as an action for support), the nullity of a void marriage may be passed upon by the court directly in that same proceeding, without need of a separate nullity action.

Exceptions to the Marriage License Requirement (Art. 34)

Niñal v. Bayadog
GR 133778 (2000)

The Art. 34 exemption from the license requirement does not apply where a legal impediment (here, a subsisting prior marriage) existed during any part of the claimed cohabitation period; the marriage is void for lack of a license, and — because it is void, not merely voidable — its validity may be questioned even after a party's death, by those with a property interest in doing so.

Borja-Manzano v. Sanchez
AM MTJ-00-1329 (2001)

A solemnizing judge who knew, or should have verified, that a party invoking the Art. 34 exemption was still validly married to someone else is administratively liable for disregarding facts that defeated the exemption.

Republic v. Dayot
GR 175581 (2008)

A false statement in the joint affidavit of cohabitation (claiming five years of cohabitation when the parties had lived together only a few months) cannot substitute for genuine compliance with Art. 34; the marriage is void ab initio for lack of an actual license.

Marriage Ceremony

Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto
GR 247576 (2021)

In seeking judicial recognition of a foreign divorce or foreign marriage status, the petitioner must prove both the foreign decree/certifications and the underlying foreign law under Rules 132 and 108 of the Rules of Court.

Office of the Dep. Ombudsman v. Llauder
GR 219062 (2020)

Public officers and solemnizing officials who participate in or facilitate irregular marriage procedures without ensuring compliance with legal requisites commit grave misconduct and gross neglect of duty.

Non-Essential Requisite: Marriage Certificate

Delgado v. Rustia
GR 155733 (2006)

The marriage certificate is documentary evidence of a marriage's existence, not one of its essential or formal requisites; its absence or loss does not, by itself, invalidate an otherwise valid marriage, which may be proven by other competent evidence.

Anonuevo v. Intestate of Rodolfo Jalandoni
GR 178221 (2010)

While a marriage certificate is the best evidence of a marriage, its absence is not fatal; the existence of a valid marriage may be established by secondary evidence, such as testimonies of witnesses, birth certificates of children, and open cohabitation as husband and wife.

Foreign Divorce

Garcia v. Recio
GR 138322 (2001)

Both the fact of a foreign divorce decree and the foreign law authorizing it must be properly pleaded and proven, under the ordinary rules of evidence, before Philippine courts will give it effect — mere assertion or an unauthenticated document is insufficient.

Amor-Catalan v. Court of Appeals
GR 167109 (2007)

An interested party (such as a first spouse whose marriage is affected) has legal standing to file a petition to declare a subsequent bigamous marriage void, even when foreign divorce decrees are involved.

Vda. de Catalan v. Catalan-Lee
GR 183622 (2012)

Under Article 26(2) of the Family Code, a party alleging a foreign divorce must present official proof of both the foreign decree and the foreign law authorizing the alien spouse to remarry; failure to prove foreign law prevents Philippine courts from recognizing the divorce.

Fujiki v. Marinay
GR 196049 (2013)

A.M. 02-11-10-SC's restriction that only the husband or wife may file a nullity petition does not bar a Filipino's first husband from seeking recognition of a foreign judgment that nullified his wife's bigamous second marriage; such recognition may proceed as a special proceeding under Rule 108, without a full trial-type nullity case, respecting the foreign court's jurisdiction.

Republic v. Manalo
GR 221029 (24 April 2018)

Article 26, paragraph 2's benefit — capacitating the Filipino spouse to remarry after a foreign divorce — extends even where it is the Filipino spouse who personally initiates and obtains the divorce abroad, not only where the alien spouse does so; this avoids the anomaly of the Filipino remaining bound while the alien spouse is free to remarry.

Galapon v. Republic
GR 243722 (22 Jan 2020)

Extending the Manalo doctrine, Article 26(2) of the Family Code applies whether the foreign divorce was obtained through a judicial contest or by mutual agreement, as long as the divorce validly severs the marriage under the alien spouse's national law.

Republic v. Bayog-Saito
GR 247297 (17 Aug 2022)

Pursuant to the Republic v. Manalo ruling, a foreign divorce obtained by or with the involvement of a Filipino spouse may be judicially recognized under Rule 108 upon proper proof of the foreign decree and the alien spouse's national law.

Impugning Validity of a Marriage & Necessity of Court Declaration

Enrico v. Heirs of Medinaceli
GR 173614 (2007)

Under A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC, for marriages celebrated under the Family Code, only the husband or the wife can file a direct petition for declaration of absolute nullity; heirs cannot file such direct petition during or after the spouses' lifetime (though they may attack it collaterally in estate settlement).

Carlos v. Sandoval
GR 179922 (2008)

The restriction in A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC (limiting standing to file a petition for declaration of nullity strictly to the husband or wife) applies exclusively to marriages celebrated under the Family Code, not to marriages contracted under the Civil Code.

Ablaza v. Republic
GR 158298 (2010)

For marriages celebrated prior to the effectivity of the Family Code, an interested third party (such as a sibling with a legal/property interest) has standing to file a direct action for declaration of nullity of a void marriage, as A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC does not operate retroactively.

Bolos v. Bolos
GR 186400 (2010)

A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC applies prospectively and exclusively to marriages solemnized under the Family Code; it does not govern actions for nullity of marriages celebrated under the Civil Code.

Juliano-Llave v. Republic
GR 169766 (2011)

In marriages solemnized under the Civil Code, any interested party whose property or legal rights are directly affected (including a bigamous second spouse or heirs) has legal standing to question the validity of a void marriage even after the death of one of the spouses.

Yu v. Carpio / Yu v. Reyes-Carpio
GR 189207 (2011)

The trial court retains jurisdiction to issue provisional orders regarding support pendente lite and child custody during the pendency of a petition for declaration of nullity, even while an interlocutory appeal or certiorari petition is pending.

Void Marriages

Republic v. Court of Appeals & Castro
GR 103047 (1994)

Laid down the requisites for a judicial declaration of presumptive death under Art. 41: the absentee spouse's absence for the statutory period, a well-founded belief the absentee is dead, an intent to remarry, and a summary proceeding for the declaration.

Republic v. Cantor
GR 184621 (2010)

"Well-founded belief" requires diligent and reasonable efforts to locate the absent spouse — the present spouse's efforts (a few inquiries, no sustained search) were found insufficient, denying the presumptive-death petition.

Republic v. Quiñonez
GR 237412 (2020)

Reinforced the Cantor standard: the present spouse must show active, not merely passive, search efforts to establish a well-founded belief of death before remarriage may be allowed under Art. 41.

Santos v. Court of Appeals
GR 112019 (1995, en banc)

First laid down the three classic requisites of Article 36 psychological incapacity: gravity, juridical antecedence (rooted in the party's history before the marriage), and incurability.

Republic v. Court of Appeals and Molina
GR 108763 (1997, en banc)

Laid down strict guidelines (commonly cited as eight) for proving psychological incapacity, including a requirement of expert psychiatric/psychological testimony and OSG certification — a restrictive framework that made Art. 36 petitions difficult to win for over two decades.

Tan-Andal v. Andal
GR 196359, en banc (2021)

Abandoned Molina's strict medical framework: psychological incapacity is now a legal, not a purely medical, concept, provable by clear and convincing evidence — including credible ordinary-witness testimony describing an enduring personality structure — without mandatorily requiring expert psychiatric testimony.

Clavecilla v. Clavecilla
GR 228127 (6 March 2023)

Either spouse — whether or not he or she is the psychologically incapacitated one — may initiate an Art. 36 petition; the doctrine of unclean hands does not bar the incapacitated spouse himself from seeking the declaration.

Candelario v. Candelario
GR 222068 (25 July 2023)

Article 36 may be applied retroactively even to marriages celebrated before the Family Code's effectivity (3 August 1988), consistent with Article 256, provided no vested or acquired rights are thereby prejudiced.

Calderon v. Roxas
GR 185595 (2013)

Support pendente lite may be enforced even while a petition for certiorari challenging the propriety of that support order is still pending before a higher court — the interlocutory support order is not automatically stayed.

Declaration of Nullity & Bigamy

Nollora v. People
GR 191425 (2011)

A bigamy conviction was upheld despite the accused's claim that his conversion to Islam allowed polygamy; he failed to prove compliance with the specific requirements of the Code of Muslim Personal Laws for a valid subsequent marriage under that law, so the general (monogamous) marriage law and the bigamy provision applied.

Zamoranos v. People
GR 193902 (2011)

A bigamy case was dismissed where both the accused's marriages were shown to have been contracted and solemnized under Muslim law (which permits polygamy for Muslims), placing the matter within the jurisdiction of the Shari'a courts rather than supporting a criminal bigamy charge in the regular courts.

Voidable Marriages

Almelor v. Regional Trial Court
GR 179620 (2008)

A husband's undisclosed homosexuality does not, by itself, fall within the specific fraud grounds enumerated in Art. 46 for annulment, nor was it independently proven to satisfy the gravity/juridical-antecedence/incurability test for Art. 36 psychological incapacity on the evidence presented.

Procedure & Effects of Termination of Marriage

Tuason v. Court of Appeals
GR 116607 (1996)

A decision annulling a marriage for psychological incapacity, rendered after the respondent-husband was declared in default for failing to answer despite notice, was not set aside on his later petition for relief from judgment, since he failed to show the extrinsic fraud or excusable negligence needed to disturb a final judgment.

Ancheta v. Ancheta
GR 145370 (2004)

A judgment declaring a marriage void may itself be annulled for extrinsic fraud where a spouse was deprived of a real opportunity to participate in the proceeding (e.g., defective or evasive service of summons/notice), since due process was denied.

Yu v. YuUNVERIFIED
GR 164915 (2006)

Not independently re-verified this session; understood to concern interim custody/support arrangements pending resolution of a nullity case. Confirm directly.

Family Code, Title 2: Legal Separation (Arts. 55–67)

Pacete v. Carriaga, Jr.
GR L-53880 (1994)

A legal separation decree cannot rest on a default judgment alone; even where the defendant-spouse is declared in default, the plaintiff must still present evidence in a full-blown trial, since collusion between the spouses to obtain the decree is presumed absent proof to the contrary, and the State must actively ensure no such collusion exists.

Lerma v. Court of AppealsUNVERIFIED
GR L-33352 (1974)

Not independently re-verified this session; understood to concern a wife's adultery as a ground for legal separation and related support pendente lite issues. Confirm directly.

Sabalones v. Court of AppealsUNVERIFIED
GR 106169 (1994)

Not independently re-verified this session; understood to concern a court's authority to issue protective/injunctive orders over conjugal property while a legal separation case remains pending. Confirm directly.

Lapuz Sy v. Eufemio
GR L-30977 (1972)

An action for legal separation is purely personal to the spouses; it does not survive and must be dismissed if a party dies before final judgment, since the death itself already dissolves the marriage, mooting the case.

Macadangdang v. Court of AppealsUNVERIFIED
GR L-49542 (1980)

Not independently re-verified this session — confirm the precise holding directly against the assigned reading.

Siochi v. GozonUNVERIFIED
