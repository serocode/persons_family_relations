Part One — Preliminary Title, Chapter 1: Effect & Application of Laws

When Laws Take Effect (CC 2)

Tañada v. Tuvera
GR 63915 (29 Dec 1986)

Publication of laws in the Official Gazette is indispensable to due process. A clause stating a law is effective "immediately" only fixes the reference point for counting from publication — it does not waive the publication requirement itself.

Villafuerte v. Cordial, Jr.
GR 222450 (7 July 2020)

Not every issuance needs publication. Interpretative regulations and purely internal rules — regulating only agency personnel, not the public, such as letters of instruction to subordinates on how to perform their duties — need not be published to take effect or to be valid.

Judicial Decisions: Stare Decisis (CC 8)

De Roy v. Court of Appeals
GR 80718 (1988)

A Supreme Court decision forms part of the legal system from the date of its promulgation; a party's or counsel's unawareness of a doctrine because it had not yet appeared in the official reports (SCRA) is not an excuse.

Pesca v. Pesca
GR 136921 (17 April 2001)

The guidelines in Republic v. Molina for proving Article 36 psychological incapacity may be applied even to a petition filed before Molina was decided, since judicial interpretation of a law forms part of that law as of the date of its enactment, not the date of the interpreting decision.

Duty to Render Judgment (CC 9, 10)

Alonzo v. Intermediate Appellate Court
GR L-72873 (28 May 1987)

Where a co-owner had actual, undisputed knowledge of a sale, this may be treated as equivalent to the written notice the law requires to start the period for legal redemption — the Court applied the law's underlying purpose over its strict letter.

People v. Tulagan
GR 227363 (12 March 2019)

Harmonized the overlapping and previously inconsistent penalty schemes of the Revised Penal Code and R.A. 7610 for sexual abuse/lascivious conduct against minors of different age brackets, laying down a definitive penalty matrix where the statutes' interplay had been unclear.

Conflicts Rules (CC 14–18) — Territoriality, Nationality, Lex Situs, Renvoi

Del Socorro v. Van Wilsem
GR 193707 (10 Dec 2014)

R.A. 9262 (VAWC) is a territorial penal statute enforceable against a foreign national father for denying support to his child in the Philippines; absent proof of a contrary Dutch law, the processual presumption (foreign law presumed same as Philippine law) applies.

IPAMS v. De Vera
GR 205703 (7 March 2016)

Party autonomy to stipulate a foreign law governing an overseas Filipino worker's employment contract is not absolute; it must be expressly and validly agreed upon and cannot be used to render Philippine labor-protection laws illusory (citing Pakistan International Airlines and Sameer Overseas).

Aznar v. Garcia (the "Christensen" case)
7 SCRA 95 (1963)

The classic renvoi case: California's own conflicts rule pointed the question of succession back to the law of the decedent's domicile (the Philippines), and the Philippine court accepted that referral back, applying Philippine internal law on legitimes.

Raytheon International v. Rouzie
GR 162894 (26 Feb 2008)

The mere presence of foreign elements in a case does not automatically warrant dismissal on the ground of forum non conveniens; it is a matter of sound judicial discretion, not an automatic bar, where the Philippine court has jurisdiction and trial here is not seriously inconvenient.

Roehr v. Rodriguez
GR 142820 (2003)

Custodio v. Court of Appeals
GR 116100 (9 Feb 1996)

Constructing a wall that legally blocks a neighbor's former passage, without malice or bad faith, is damnum absque injuria (loss without legal injury) — the exercise of one's own property rights is not actionable under Arts. 19–21 absent bad faith or intent to harm.

Duty to Act with Justice, Honesty & Good Faith

Cebu Country Club v. Elizagaque
GR 160273 (18 Jan 2008)

A private club's board may have absolute discretion to blackball a membership applicant under its by-laws, but exercising that discretion in bad faith and without giving the applicant any reason violates Art. 19's duty to act with justice and good faith, giving rise to liability.

Actions for Breach of Promise to Marry

Baksh v. Court of Appeals
GR 97336 (1993)

A mere breach of promise to marry is not actionable per se, but where the promise is attended by fraud and deceit, employed solely to obtain sexual favors, with willful intent to injure the woman's honor and reputation, damages are proper under Art. 21.

Guevara v. BanachUNVERIFIED
GR 214016, 3rd Div., Leonen, J. (24 Nov 2021)

Could not locate a reliable digest — confirm directly (likely a further refinement of the Baksh/Wassmer line on breach of promise to marry, given the placement).

Wassmer v. Velez
GR L-20089 (1964)

The classic case: a groom who abandoned the bride two days before an elaborately-prepared wedding, without justification, was held liable for damages under Art. 21 — not for the broken promise itself, but for the manner (bad faith, contrary to good customs) in which the wrong was done.

Tortious Interference in a Contract

So Ping Bun v. Court of Appeals
GR 120554 (1999)

Interference with another's contract is not automatically tortious; a party who interferes to protect his own legitimate financial interest, without employing wrongful means, is not liable — lawful competition and self-interest can justify what would otherwise look like interference.

Human Personality (Privacy)

RCPI v. Verchez
GR 164349 (2006)

A telegraph company's negligent delay in transmitting an urgent message about a family member's worsening illness — causing the family to fail to reach her in time before her death — was actionable; the negligence, though arising from a contract of carriage of messages, supported liability for the resulting anguish under the Human Relations provisions.

Hing v. Choachuy
GR 179736 (2013)

Installing CCTV cameras aimed at a neighboring property, capturing activities within a reasonable expectation of privacy, violates the right to privacy under Art. 26 and supports an injunction, even absent an existing legal easement dispute between the parties.

Liability of Public Officers

Aberca v. Ver
GR L-69866 (1988)

Military and other public officers who violate a person's constitutional rights (illegal searches, arbitrary detention during Martial Law-era security operations) may be held civilly liable under Arts. 32 and 19–21, and cannot hide behind a superior's order or a state-of-emergency claim to escape liability.

Lim v. Ponce de LeonUNVERIFIED
L-22554 (29 Aug 1975)

Could not locate a reliable digest — confirm directly (likely an Art. 32 illegal-seizure liability case against a public officer, given the era and placement).

Tabuena v. Sandiganbayan
GR 103501-03 (1997)

A public officer who, in good faith, complies with a direct, facially lawful order from a superior (in this case, a presidential directive regarding a COA-audited disbursement) may be acquitted of malversation/graft where the element of intent to gain or bad faith is not independently proven.

Unfair Competition

Willaware v. Jesichris
GR 195549 (2014)

Unfair competition targets the use of unjust, oppressive, or high-handed methods that deprive others of a fair chance to compete — ordinary lawful competition, even if it takes away a rival's customers, is not by itself actionable.

Legal Capacity: Sex

Silverio v. Republic
GR 174689 (2007)

There is no Philippine law authorizing a change of the "sex" entry in the civil registry based on sex reassignment surgery; "sex" for these purposes is fixed at birth in its ordinary, biological sense, absent specific legislation.

Republic v. Cagandahan
GR 166676 (2008)

Distinguished from Silverio: where the sexual ambiguity is congenital (here, Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia) rather than surgically created, the person may, upon reaching majority, choose which sex to be legally identified with.

Legal Impediments: Prior/Subsequent Marriages

Wiegel v. Sempio-Diy
GR L-53703 (1986)

Under the old (pre-Family Code) rule, a marriage void from the beginning (e.g., due to a subsisting prior marriage) does not need a prior judicial declaration of nullity before a party can be treated as never having been validly married for certain purposes — its validity may be passed upon directly in the very proceeding where it is raised.

Ty v. Court of Appeals
GR 127406 (2000)

Similarly, a party sued for support could still have her marriage's validity (void for want of a license) passed upon by the court in that same proceeding, without needing a separate, prior action for declaration of nullity — decided under the old rule before Art. 40's stricter requirement took full effect for remarriage purposes.

Pasamonte v. TenezaUNVERIFIED
AC 11104 (2020)

Could not locate a reliable digest — confirm directly (an administrative/disbarment case, given the "AC" docket number).

Consent

Republic v. Albios
GR 198780 (2013)

A marriage entered into so a Filipino spouse could obtain a foreign immigration benefit remains valid, since consent under Art. 2 was freely given in the proper form; a marriage is not automatically void merely because the underlying motive was to obtain an incidental benefit.

Solemnizing Officers

Beso v. Daguman
AM MTJ-99-1211 (2000)

A judge who solemnizes a marriage outside his territorial jurisdiction does not thereby void the marriage — the marriage remains valid, but the judge is administratively liable for the irregularity.

Aranes v. Occiano
AM MTJ-02-1390 (2002)

A judge who solemnizes a marriage knowing the couple has not yet obtained a marriage license — proceeding anyway based on a promise to submit it later — renders the marriage void for absence of a license at the time of the ceremony, and is himself administratively liable.

OCA v. Necessario
AM MTJ-07-1691 (2013)

The "package marriage" scandal case: judges solemnizing marriages en masse without personally verifying compliance with the essential and formal requisites (proper licenses, actual appearance, genuine Art. 34 exemption) were held administratively liable for gross neglect and gross ignorance of the law.

Marriage License

Sy v. Court of Appeals
GR 127263 (2000)

Irregularities surrounding the issuance of a marriage license (discrepancies in venue/date) do not void a marriage where a license did in fact exist and was used — courts lean toward the presumption of a valid marriage absent proof the license was altogether absent.

Alcantara v. Alcantara
GR 167746 (2007)

A discrepancy in the marriage license number appearing on the marriage contract does not void the marriage absent clear proof, from the local civil registrar, that no license was actually issued to the parties.

Abbas v. Abbas
GR 183896 (2013)

A civil registrar's certification of "no record" of a marriage license is insufficient to prove the license never existed unless the certification affirmatively shows an exhaustive search of all pertinent records was conducted.

De Castro v. De Castro
GR 160172 (2008)

Article 40's requirement of a prior judicial declaration of nullity applies specifically for purposes of remarriage; for other purposes (such as an action for support), the nullity of a void marriage may be passed upon by the court directly in that same proceeding, without need of a separate nullity action.

Exceptions to the Marriage License Requirement (Art. 34)

Niñal v. Bayadog
GR 133778 (2000)

The Art. 34 exemption from the license requirement does not apply where a legal impediment (here, a subsisting prior marriage) existed during any part of the claimed cohabitation period; the marriage is void for lack of a license, and — because it is void, not merely voidable — its validity may be questioned even after a party's death, by those with a property interest in doing so.

Borja-Manzano v. Sanchez
AM MTJ-00-1329 (2001)

A solemnizing judge who knew, or should have verified, that a party invoking the Art. 34 exemption was still validly married to someone else is administratively liable for disregarding facts that defeated the exemption.

Republic v. Dayot
GR 175581 (2008)

A false statement in the joint affidavit of cohabitation (claiming five years of cohabitation when the parties had lived together only a few months) cannot substitute for genuine compliance with Art. 34; the marriage is void ab initio for lack of an actual license.

Foreign Divorce

Garcia v. Recio
GR 138322 (2001)

Both the fact of a foreign divorce decree and the foreign law authorizing it must be properly pleaded and proven, under the ordinary rules of evidence, before Philippine courts will give it effect — mere assertion or an unauthenticated document is insufficient.

Fujiki v. Marinay
GR 196049 (2013)

A.M. 02-11-10-SC's restriction that only the husband or wife may file a nullity petition does not bar a Filipino's first husband from seeking recognition of a foreign judgment that nullified his wife's bigamous second marriage; such recognition may proceed as a special proceeding under Rule 108, without a full trial-type nullity case, respecting the foreign court's jurisdiction.

Republic v. Manalo

Void Marriages

Republic v. Court of Appeals & Castro
GR 103047 (1994)

Laid down the requisites for a judicial declaration of presumptive death under Art. 41: the absentee spouse's absence for the statutory period, a well-founded belief the absentee is dead, an intent to remarry, and a summary proceeding for the declaration.

Republic v. Cantor
GR 184621 (2010)

"Well-founded belief" requires diligent and reasonable efforts to locate the absent spouse — the present spouse's efforts (a few inquiries, no sustained search) were found insufficient, denying the presumptive-death petition.

Republic v. Quiñonez
GR 237412 (2020)

Reinforced the Cantor standard: the present spouse must show active, not merely passive, search efforts to establish a well-founded belief of death before remarriage may be allowed under Art. 41.

Santos v. Court of Appeals
GR 112019 (1995, en banc)

First laid down the three classic requisites of Article 36 psychological incapacity: gravity, juridical antecedence (rooted in the party's history before the marriage), and incurability.

Republic v. Court of Appeals and Molina
GR 108763 (1997, en banc)

Laid down strict guidelines (commonly cited as eight) for proving psychological incapacity, including a requirement of expert psychiatric/psychological testimony and OSG certification — a restrictive framework that made Art. 36 petitions difficult to win for over two decades.

Tan-Andal v. Andal
GR 196359, en banc (2021)

Abandoned Molina's strict medical framework: psychological incapacity is now a legal, not a purely medical, concept, provable by clear and convincing evidence — including credible ordinary-witness testimony describing an enduring personality structure — without mandatorily requiring expert psychiatric testimony.

Clavecilla v. Clavecilla
GR 228127 (6 March 2023)

Either spouse — whether or not he or she is the psychologically incapacitated one — may initiate an Art. 36 petition; the doctrine of unclean hands does not bar the incapacitated spouse himself from seeking the declaration.

Candelario v. Candelario
GR 222068 (25 July 2023)

Article 36 may be applied retroactively even to marriages celebrated before the Family Code's effectivity (3 August 1988), consistent with Article 256, provided no vested or acquired rights are thereby prejudiced.

Calderon v. Roxas
GR 185595 (2013)

Support pendente lite may be enforced even while a petition for certiorari challenging the propriety of that support order is still pending before a higher court — the interlocutory support order is not automatically stayed.

Declaration of Nullity & Bigamy

Nollora v. People
GR 191425 (2011)

A bigamy conviction was upheld despite the accused's claim that his conversion to Islam allowed polygamy; he failed to prove compliance with the specific requirements of the Code of Muslim Personal Laws for a valid subsequent marriage under that law, so the general (monogamous) marriage law and the bigamy provision applied.

Zamoranos v. People
GR 193902 (2011)

A bigamy case was dismissed where both the accused's marriages were shown to have been contracted and solemnized under Muslim law (which permits polygamy for Muslims), placing the matter within the jurisdiction of the Shari'a courts rather than supporting a criminal bigamy charge in the regular courts.

Voidable Marriages

Almelor v. Regional Trial Court
GR 179620 (2008)

A husband's undisclosed homosexuality does not, by itself, fall within the specific fraud grounds enumerated in Art. 46 for annulment, nor was it independently proven to satisfy the gravity/juridical-antecedence/incurability test for Art. 36 psychological incapacity on the evidence presented.

Procedure & Effects of Termination of Marriage

Tuason v. Court of Appeals
GR 116607 (1996)

A decision annulling a marriage for psychological incapacity, rendered after the respondent-husband was declared in default for failing to answer despite notice, was not set aside on his later petition for relief from judgment, since he failed to show the extrinsic fraud or excusable negligence needed to disturb a final judgment.

Ancheta v. Ancheta
GR 145370 (2004)

A judgment declaring a marriage void may itself be annulled for extrinsic fraud where a spouse was deprived of a real opportunity to participate in the proceeding (e.g., defective or evasive service of summons/notice), since due process was denied.

Family Code, Title 2: Legal Separation (Arts. 55–67)

GR L-53880 (1994)

A legal separation decree cannot rest on a default judgment alone; even where the defendant-spouse is declared in default, the plaintiff must still present evidence in a full-blown trial, since collusion between the spouses to obtain the decree is presumed absent proof to the contrary, and the State must actively ensure no such collusion exists.
